Bathroom Remodeling

Accessible Bathroom & Walk-In Shower Planning for Your Home

A walk-in shower is not automatically an accessible shower. The right layout depends on how someone enters, turns, reaches the controls and gets assistance if needed. Start with those tasks before choosing a curbless detail or buying fixtures.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below

Design for the person and the route into the room

Discuss current and anticipated needs with the household and, where useful, an occupational therapist or other appropriate adviser. Bring the dimensions of any mobility equipment. Consider the approach from the bedroom, the bathroom doorway, maneuvering space and the position of doors and drawers.

A larger shower can make the rest of a small bathroom harder to use. Draw the whole room, including the toilet and vanity, then test the sequence of getting in, closing the door, using fixtures and leaving. A taped layout can expose conflicts that a product photograph will not.

Use accessibility guidance with the right scope

The U.S. Access Board: Bathing Rooms illustrates relationships among clearances, seats, grab bars and shower controls. It addresses ADA-covered facilities; it is not evidence that every private-home remodel must follow every ADA dimension. Have the designer identify which local requirements apply and which additional features suit the household.

A label such as “ADA-style” on a product does not establish compliance of the room. Installation location, surrounding space and the complete arrangement matter.

Coordinate the shower entry with the floor construction

For a curbless or low-threshold entry, establish the available floor depth, structural constraints, drain location and water-containment approach. Work out the shower slope and the transition to the rest of the bathroom before choosing large floor tiles or moving framing.

Also plan where spray goes when a door is open, whether a seat is needed and how the enclosure affects assistance. The glass layout should support daily use rather than forcing the user to reach around a fixed panel to turn on the water.

Place support and controls before closing the walls

Discuss grab-bar locations and suitable backing while the walls are accessible. Coordinate those locations with valves, wiring, niches and the glass enclosure. Keep dimensioned photos of reinforcement for future work; do not rely on memory after the tile is installed.

Test the reach to controls from the intended standing or seated position. Consider a handheld shower, reachable storage, lighting at the entry and switches that are easy to operate. Select floor materials for their intended wet use and cleaning needs; no finish eliminates the need to manage water on the floor.

Decide what to build now and what to prepare for

Some features need to be installed now to make the room usable. Others, such as planned reinforcement or an adaptable storage arrangement, may support later changes. Record those choices in the drawings and budget so future adaptability is intentional.

Before approving the layout, walk through it with the people who will use or help maintain it. Their feedback on reach, transfers, privacy and cleaning is more useful than a generic accessibility checklist alone.

Questions homeowners ask

Does every accessible bathroom need a curbless shower?

No single feature fits every household. Entry height, available space, transfer needs and construction constraints should be evaluated together. A curbless entry can help in some situations but does not make an otherwise cramped room accessible.

Can grab bars be added later?

Often, but their anchorage and location need to suit the wall and user. Planning reinforcement during a remodel can make later work easier. Have a qualified installer verify the actual construction before installation.

Sources and local requirements
