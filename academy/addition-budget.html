ADUs & Home Additions

Planning a San Diego Home Addition: Budget, Structure & Schedule

A home addition has two jobs: create useful new space and connect it properly to the house you already own. The connection often explains why a simple square-foot estimate misses important work. Plan the old and new construction together before comparing prices.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below
In this guide
  • Define the room and its connection
  • Investigate the foundation and site
  • Resolve the roof before promising an interior ceiling
  • Build a complete budget rather than one rate
  • Sequence the work around dependency points

Define the room and its connection

Draw how people will enter the addition, where furniture will sit and which existing room changes to make it work. A new bedroom may also require a hallway, relocated window or revised heating distribution. Include those changes in the scope rather than pricing only the new footprint.

Compare an addition with a rearrangement of existing space if both could solve the problem. Evaluate useful area, daylight and circulation—not just the amount of floor area added.

Investigate the foundation and site

Have the designer identify the survey, soil and existing-foundation information needed for the proposal. The old foundation’s capacity and the new work’s connection cannot be established by visual similarity alone. List required investigation before committing to a foundation assumption.

In the City of San Diego, the building-permit guidance directs homeowners to residential addition requirements. Grading and other approvals may also apply to the site. Start with the responsible jurisdiction, not a County-wide assumption. City of San Diego: Building Permits and Residential Additions.

Resolve the roof before promising an interior ceiling

Show how roof geometry, runoff and weather protection connect at the existing house. Ask the designer to coordinate the exterior roof line with the ceiling height you expect indoors. A convenient roof connection can still produce an awkward low ceiling or drainage problem if those decisions are made separately.

Identify how the existing home will be protected while the opening is made. Temporary weather protection, repairs to the old roof and restoration of adjoining finishes belong in the estimate.

Build a complete budget rather than one rate

  • Design, investigation, engineering and authority fees.
  • Access, demolition, earthwork and foundation construction.
  • Framing, roofing, windows and exterior weather protection.
  • Electrical, plumbing and heating or cooling extensions.
  • Interior finishes and repairs within the existing house.
  • Site restoration and a reserve for unresolved conditions.

Ask which items are priced, allowances or exclusions. Do not compare a shell-only addition price with a finished-room proposal.

Sequence the work around dependency points

Track design and approvals, procurement, foundation work, framing, weather enclosure, rough systems, inspections and finishes. The exact order depends on the design, but the schedule should show what must be verified before concealment and when the old house becomes exposed.

If you will remain at home, identify the periods when access, privacy or utilities are affected. A smaller addition can still disrupt the existing kitchen or bathroom if its connection passes through that space.

Questions homeowners ask

Can an addition use the same foundation detail as a nearby house?

Do not assume so. Site conditions, loads and existing construction need project-specific evaluation.

Is a price per square foot enough to set the budget?

It can be an early reference only when its scope is clear. Separate the site work and existing-house repairs to understand what the figure leaves out.
