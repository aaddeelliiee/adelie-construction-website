Planning & Budgeting

Remodel Allowances, Contingency & Budget Tracking Explained

An allowance is money assigned to something not fully selected or priced. A contingency is a reserve for uncertainty. Neither makes a vague proposal complete. Before comparing remodel bids, establish what each amount covers and how it changes when the real cost is known.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below
In this guide
  • Ask what is inside each allowance
  • Keep the contingency tied to identified risks
  • Count the expenses outside the construction price
  • Use one current forecast
  • Approve the change before the changed work

Ask what is inside each allowance

A tile allowance might cover only the tile purchase, or it might include other items. Ask whether tax, delivery, waste, installation materials and labor are inside the amount. Clarify how credits, overages and any agreed markup are calculated.

Illustrative example: a contract carries a $3,000 material allowance. The selected material costs $3,600 on the same agreed basis. The material difference is $600; it is not automatically the full change if freight, tax or installation also differs. This is an arithmetic example, not an ADELIE price or a recommended allowance.

Use actual selections or supplier quotes to test whether an allowance is realistic before signing. A low placeholder can make one bid look cheaper while leaving the same final purchase unresolved.

Keep the contingency tied to identified risks

List unresolved conditions such as concealed piping, uncertain floor preparation or a utility connection awaiting investigation. Set a reserve with the project team based on those risks and the stage of design. There is no single percentage that makes every remodel adequately funded.

Track the reserve separately from the construction contract. As an unknown becomes defined, record the decision and revised forecast. Do not treat unused reserve as finish-upgrade money while significant investigations remain open.

Count the expenses outside the construction price

Check design, engineering, permits, testing, association charges, temporary accommodation and owner-purchased products. Decide who pays each item and whether it appears elsewhere in the budget. Avoid adding it twice simply because both the contractor and homeowner have mentioned it.

For each quote, label exclusions explicitly. “Owner supplies appliances” should prompt questions about delivery, storage, installation, connections and damage responsibility. The product purchase is only one part of the arrangement.

Use one current forecast

Track these amounts separately: original contract, approved additions and credits, pending decisions, owner purchases, professional fees and remaining reserve. Record payments as a separate cash-flow column; money paid to date is not the same as final project cost.

A useful selection log includes the item, allowance basis, selected price, related labor difference, approval date and order deadline. Updating that log before an order is placed gives you a chance to choose a less expensive option.

Approve the change before the changed work

CSLB’s home-improvement contract guidance calls for written change orders signed before changes to the work. Have the document identify the scope and price adjustment and explain the schedule effect. A verbal selection conversation is not a complete change record. CSLB: Home Improvement Contracts.

If a price is still unknown, agree on the investigation or next limited step rather than approving an open-ended promise that everything will be settled later.

Questions homeowners ask

Is an allowance an extra charge on top of the contract?

It depends on how the contract is written, but an allowance commonly sits within the stated price. Confirm that explicitly and establish how the final amount is reconciled.

Should I choose the bid with the smallest contingency?

A reserve is not a measure of contractor quality. Compare the scope, known selections and unresolved risks. A small reserve does not remove those risks, and a large one does not excuse an incomplete estimate.
