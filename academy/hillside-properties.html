San Diego Planning Considerations

Remodeling a San Diego Hillside Home: Drainage, Access and Ground Work

On a hillside property, the work outside the room can determine what is practical inside it. An addition, deck or new opening may involve foundations, retaining structures, equipment access and drainage that a floor plan alone cannot show.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below
In this guide
  • Start with the site information you already have
  • Check land-use and grading constraints early
  • Treat drainage as a connected system
  • Budget access and temporary work
  • Keep an inspection and maintenance record

Start with the site information you already have

Collect surveys, previous soils or geotechnical reports, drainage plans and records for retaining walls or additions. Mark visible cracks, recurring wet areas and places where runoff collects. These observations help define an investigation; they do not establish the cause.

Ask the design team which existing records are usable and what new evaluation the proposed work needs. A neighboring property’s solution is not a substitute for understanding your own site.

Check land-use and grading constraints early

The City of San Diego’s City of San Diego: Grading Permits identifies review considerations including environmentally sensitive lands. Steep hillsides, coastal bluffs and other mapped constraints can affect the approval path. Confirm the parcel and scope before assuming ordinary building review is sufficient.

Have the project team establish whether grading review, geotechnical input or other studies are needed. Keep this step ahead of commitments to an addition footprint or a large paved area.

Treat drainage as a connected system

Locate roof discharge, surface runoff routes, drains and any known subsurface systems. Ask where water goes now and where it will go after the project. New paving or a roof addition can change the amount and path of runoff.

A qualified civil or geotechnical professional may be needed to evaluate the site and an acceptable discharge approach. Do not redirect water toward neighboring property, a slope or a retaining wall on the assumption that moving it away from the house solves the problem.

Budget access and temporary work

Discuss how equipment and materials reach the work area, where excavated material goes and how the existing home stays accessible. Include temporary protection and restoration in the proposal rather than assuming they are included in a foundation price.

If the design relies on retaining or supporting soil, identify the responsible designer and inspection requirements. Ask what must be completed before excavation begins and which conditions could pause the work.

Keep an inspection and maintenance record

Photograph concealed drainage routes and retain approved plans, reports and inspection records. At handover, ask which drains and accessible components need routine attention and what changes should trigger professional review.

New or worsening movement, recurring water entry or damaged retaining structures deserve investigation. Covering the symptoms with landscaping or a new finish can make the underlying issue harder to assess.

Questions homeowners ask

Does every hillside remodel require new geotechnical work?

Not every project has the same scope. The design professional and permitting authority should establish what information is needed for the proposed work and site.

Can a drainage repair be kept separate from an addition?

Sometimes, but coordinate both designs first. Foundations, grading and roof runoff may share the same constrained space.
