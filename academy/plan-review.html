Permits & Inspections

San Diego Remodel Permits: From Application to Final Inspection

Submitting plans, receiving a permit and passing final inspection are different milestones. A workable remodel schedule makes room for all three. Know who is responsible for each step and keep the approved documents connected to the work happening on site.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below

  • Track review comments separately from construction changes

Identify the authority and assemble the application

Confirm whether the address is handled by the City of San Diego, another city or the County. Then ask which application fits the whole scope, including structural changes and related trades. The City of San Diego: Building Permit outlines the City’s process and points applicants toward its submittal requirements.

Organize existing and proposed plans, relevant details, calculations and product information requested for the project. Do not guess a universal document checklist: a kitchen alteration and a new addition can require different material. Assign a person to coordinate the package so the floor plan and trade drawings agree.

Track review comments separately from construction changes

Keep the reviewers’ comments with the revised drawings and the response identifying how each item was addressed. Confirm that the team is using the latest submitted set. The City’s City of San Diego: Permit Status Definitions helps distinguish review, issuance and inspection statuses.

An application accepted for review is not an issued permit. Before scheduling permit-dependent work, verify the actual authorization. Set tentative construction dates around unresolved review items rather than treating a hoped-for approval date as a commitment.

Build inspection pauses into the schedule

Ask the contractor to identify the inspections required by the approved scope and when work must remain exposed. San Diego’s City of San Diego: Types of Inspections describes underground, rough, insulation and final stages, among others. Not every project uses every inspection type.

Plan time to address corrections before the next trade covers the work. Keep inspection outcomes and correction notices in the project folder. If special inspections are required, confirm the separate arrangements; they do not replace the ordinary building inspections.

Keep field changes connected to the permit

If a proposed change affects the approved work, ask the designer and department how it must be documented and approved. The homeowner’s signed change order and the permit revision serve different purposes: one records the construction agreement, while the other addresses the approved design.

For example, moving an opening after framing may affect structure, electrical work and exterior details. Resolve the required review before assuming that a marked-up contractor sketch is enough for the final record.

Close the permit as well as the punch list

A cosmetic punch list and the authority’s final approval are separate records. Before calling the project complete, ask for the final inspection status, any outstanding conditions and the current approved drawings. Confirm whether a certificate of occupancy or other additional closeout document applies to the project.

Save the permit number and records with the home’s maintenance documents. A paid application fee, an issued permit or an invoice marked “final” is not a substitute for evidence of the required final approval.

Questions homeowners ask

Does every remodel need the same inspection sequence?

No. The required stages follow the permitted scope and applicable requirements. Have the contractor confirm the sequence for your project before work begins.

Can a project be physically finished while its permit remains open?

Yes. Outstanding inspections or corrections can remain even after finishes are installed. Track the permit status separately from construction progress and resolve open items with the responsible authority.
