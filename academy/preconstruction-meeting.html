Planning & Budgeting

What Happens at a Preconstruction Meeting?

The final working session before construction begins: scope, schedule, access, protection, decisions and communication.

Reviewed by ADELIE Construction · Updated July 27, 2026 · Homeowner education

The short answer

A preconstruction meeting turns the signed contract and approved plans into a practical jobsite plan. The homeowner, contractor and relevant project team review what will happen first, who is responsible for each remaining decision, how the home will be protected and how questions or changes will be handled.

It is not a ceremonial kickoff. It is the last organized opportunity to find missing information before crews, deliveries and inspections begin.

What should be reviewed

Walk through the scope in construction order rather than reading the proposal as a list. Confirm demolition limits, items to remain, owner-supplied products, long-lead materials, permit status, inspection milestones and the conditions assumed by the price.

Then cover the household logistics: work hours, parking, keys or access codes, pets, children, dust barriers, temporary utilities, bathroom access, material storage and where workers may or may not enter. Put the decisions and assigned responsibilities in written meeting notes.

Bring a decision and responsibility log

A useful log lists each unresolved item, the person responsible, the due date and the work that depends on it. Examples include appliance specifications before cabinet drawings, plumbing trim before rough-in and tile selections before substrate heights are finalized.

The contractor should also identify the regular point of contact, update frequency and approval method. Homeowners should know who can authorize cost or schedule changes and where the current drawings, selections and meeting notes will be stored.

Questions worth asking before the meeting ends

  • Is the permit issued, and are approved plans available to the field team?
  • Which materials are ordered, and which selections still have deadlines?
  • What existing conditions remain uncertain until demolition?
  • What is the first inspection milestone?
  • How will a potential change be documented and priced?
  • What does the homeowner need to remove, protect or relocate?
  • What would prevent the planned start date?

What should happen afterward

The contractor should distribute concise notes showing decisions, open items and owners. Any conflict between the meeting notes, plans and contract should be resolved in writing. If a material choice or field condition changes price or schedule, it belongs in the formal change-order process—not in an informal text thread.

A good meeting ends with a realistic start plan and fewer assumptions. It does not guarantee that concealed conditions will never arise; it establishes how the team will respond when they do.
