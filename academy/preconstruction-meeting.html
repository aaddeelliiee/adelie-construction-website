Planning & Budgeting

Preconstruction Meeting: A Homeowner’s Agenda Before Demolition

The meeting before construction should settle how the agreed project will actually run. Bring the current drawings, contract and selection list. Leave with decisions, responsible people and dates—not simply reassurance that everyone is ready.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below
In this guide
  • Start with the same documents
  • Agree on the first weeks, not just the completion date
  • Give each selection a decision date
  • Choose one route for instructions and changes
  • Walk the access and protection plan

Start with the same documents

Confirm the current drawing revision, written scope, approved changes and permit status. Identify any association conditions or other approvals that affect access or work. Remove outdated drawings from the working set so a supplier or trade does not act on a superseded detail.

List anything unresolved and decide whether it prevents the next activity. A pending paint color may not affect demolition, while an unresolved structural opening or appliance size may affect work much earlier.

Agree on the first weeks, not just the completion date

Walk through protection, deliveries, utility interruptions, demolition and investigation. Identify work that depends on an inspection, a delivery or a homeowner decision. Ask what would cause a change to the sequence and how that change will be communicated.

CSLB’s project checklist includes questions about supervision, work hours, permits and daily cleanup. Use those topics to confirm the practical arrangement for this project. CSLB: Construction Project Checklist.

Give each selection a decision date

Create a short log with the item, person deciding, information needed, approval date and ordering responsibility. Work backward from fabrication or installation rather than assuming everything can be selected shortly before it is needed.

For example, a faucet decision may affect the sink layout or countertop holes. Ask the contractor to explain those dependencies so the deadline has a reason. When a deadline moves, update the affected order and schedule rather than leaving two versions of the plan.

Choose one route for instructions and changes

Name the daily site contact and the person authorized to approve cost or scope changes. Agree where questions, photos and decisions will be recorded. A conversation with a trade on site should not silently become an instruction that changes the contract.

Set a regular progress discussion and a separate method for urgent access or property concerns. Record decisions afterward with the agreed action, owner and date. The record should be short enough to use and clear enough to resolve a later misunderstanding.

Walk the access and protection plan

Confirm entrances, keys or access codes, parking, storage, debris removal and areas the crew cannot use. Discuss children, pets, working from home and the availability of a bathroom or temporary kitchen. Identify who communicates with neighbors or the building manager when needed.

End the meeting by reading back open items and assigning the next action. Keep the notes with the project documents and revisit them when the scope or household arrangements change.

Questions homeowners ask

Who should attend?

Include the homeowner decision-maker and the person managing the construction. Bring in the designer, relevant trade or building representative when a specific unresolved issue requires their decision.

Does the meeting replace the contract?

No. It coordinates the agreed project. Any change to the agreement should follow the documented change process rather than relying only on meeting notes.
