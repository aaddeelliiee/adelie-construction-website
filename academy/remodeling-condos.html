San Diego Planning Considerations

Condo Remodeling in San Diego: HOA, Permits & Shared Systems

A condo remodel involves more than the space inside your front door. Before pricing a new kitchen or bathroom, find out which parts of the building you can alter, who approves the work and how the crew will get materials in. Those answers can change the layout and the construction schedule.

Get the association’s remodeling packet first

Ask the manager for the current architectural application, construction rules and governing documents. The California Department of Real Estate explains how CC&Rs and association rules govern common-interest developments. Use those documents to establish responsibilities; a neighbor’s past remodel is not approval for yours. California Department of Real Estate: Residential Subdivision Buyer’s Guide.

Request written answers about flooring assemblies, waterproofing submissions, contractor insurance documentation, work hours, deposits, elevator reservations and water shutoffs. Some buildings impose detailed requirements; others do not. The estimate should reflect your building’s actual rules rather than a standard condo surcharge.

For a detached home in an HOA, the same first step applies, especially for exterior work. Shared elevators and vertical plumbing stacks may be irrelevant, but association review and municipal approval still need separate attention.

Keep association approval and the permit separate

A board may approve a finish or layout while the building department still needs plans. Conversely, a permit does not settle an association’s ownership or architectural restrictions. For property inside the City of San Diego, start with its City of San Diego: Building Permit; other cities have their own departments.

Submit a consistent scope to both reviewers. If the approved association drawing retains the sink but the permit drawing moves it, resolve the difference before ordering cabinets. Keep the approval conditions with the contractor’s working set.

Check what sits behind the wall and below the floor

A proposed sink move may interact with a shared drain or a ceiling in the unit below. Before committing to it, identify the pipe route, access permissions and restoration responsibility. Ask whether structural or fire-resistance details limit penetrations. Do not assume that a wall belongs entirely to you because its painted surface is inside the unit.

Flooring deserves its own review. Ask the association for the required sound-control documentation and accepted assembly, including the underlayment and finish. A product advertised as quiet is not proof that the installed floor meets the building’s rules.

Price the route to the room

Walk the delivery route with the contractor: loading space, elevator dimensions, door openings, protected corridors and the final turn into the unit. Confirm where debris can wait and when it must leave. A large countertop may need a different seam arrangement if it cannot reach the kitchen as drawn.

Put shutoff notice periods, reserved delivery days and limited work hours into the schedule. These are practical constraints to price before work begins, not reasons to discover extra charges halfway through.

Before you authorize construction

  • Collect the written association decision and any conditions.
  • Confirm municipal permits and the responsible applicant.
  • Approve plumbing routes, flooring assembly and access arrangements.
  • Identify who coordinates building notices and who repairs common-area damage.
  • Agree on a fallback layout if a shared-system alteration is not allowed.

Questions homeowners ask

Can I start while the board is reviewing the application?

Wait for the approvals required for your scope. Have the manager identify whether any limited work can proceed separately, and confirm the permit status before scheduling that work.

Will keeping the existing layout simplify the project?

Often it reduces the number of shared-system questions, but flooring, waterproofing, electrical work and building access can still need review. Price the actual work rather than assuming that an unchanged floor plan makes it permit-free.
