Planning & Budgeting

How to Write a Remodeling Scope Before Requesting Bids

“Remodel the kitchen” leaves too much for each bidder to interpret. A useful scope explains what changes, what stays and who is responsible for the work around those boundaries. You do not need to solve every construction detail yourself, but you should give bidders the same starting information.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below

Describe the problem before listing products

Write down the daily problems the remodel must solve and rank them. More storage, a safer shower entry and better circulation are clearer goals than “make it high end.” Separate essential outcomes from preferred finishes and optional additions.

Use those priorities when comparing alternatives. Keeping a wall or an existing utility location may reduce work, but it is only a useful saving if the revised plan still solves the problem that prompted the remodel.

Mark the work boundaries room by room

On a plan or annotated photos, identify demolition, retained items and new work. Include adjoining surfaces that need restoration. If a new doorway affects flooring and paint in the next room, show where that repair ends.

List protection, disposal, utility work, permit responsibilities, inspections, cleanup and handover records. CSLB’s contract guidance addresses detailed written scope and other contract requirements; use the final agreement to resolve responsibilities rather than relying on an early wish list. CSLB: Home Improvement Contracts.

Separate selected items from allowances and unknowns

For a selected product, provide the model or an agreed specification. For an unselected item, state the allowance basis and what it covers. For an unknown condition, identify the investigation needed and how a resulting change will be considered.

For example, replacing a sink in the same cabinet is different from relocating it across the room. Give bidders the same proposed location, appliance information and known site constraints so their prices describe comparable work.

Make exclusions visible before choosing a bidder

Ask each contractor to identify work it has excluded, assumed or assigned to the homeowner. Check whether another trade, supplier or professional must provide that work. Add owner purchases and professional fees to the total project forecast even when they are not in the construction contract.

Resolve conflicting assumptions before comparing totals. One bidder may include leveling the floor while another assumes it is ready. Neither number is useful without understanding the difference.

Use a readiness check before authorizing the start

  • Are the essential goals and work boundaries agreed?
  • Are drawings and specifications ready for the next stage?
  • Are permits and other approvals identified?
  • Do selections and allowances fit the full budget?
  • Is there a plan for access, household needs and unresolved conditions?

Record alternatives as separate options rather than hiding them inside one price. Once an option is selected, update the scope and keep one dated version that everyone uses.

Questions homeowners ask

Do I need finished drawings before contacting a contractor?

Not necessarily. Early conversations can help establish feasibility and what design work is needed. Make clear that an early discussion or allowance-based estimate is not the same as a fully defined construction price.

How can I reduce cost without making the project worse?

Compare changes to complexity, quantity and work boundaries against your priorities. Ask what each alternative saves and what function or appearance it gives up before choosing it.
