Permits & Inspections

Unpermitted Work in a San Diego Home: What to Check First

If an old addition or converted garage has uncertain permit history, investigate before building your new remodel around it. The first task is to establish what was approved and what exists today. Missing paperwork alone does not prove that the work was illegal, and a permit number alone does not prove that it received final approval.

Updated October 24, 2026 · ADELIE Construction · Sources below
In this guide
  • Build a record of the existing space
  • Ask what correction route applies to this particular work
  • Leave room in the scope for investigation
  • Compare correction with redesign
  • Keep the final record, not just the application

Build a record of the existing space

Collect the address, parcel information, available permit numbers, old drawings, inspection records and dated photographs. Ask the responsible city or County department what records it holds. The City of San Diego provides a City of San Diego: Development Services Records Requests. A property in an incorporated city is not automatically handled by the County simply because it is in San Diego County.

Have the space measured and compare it with the records. Mark differences such as an added bathroom, enclosed patio, altered garage door or moved wall. Keep uncertain items visible in the project notes instead of presenting them as approved existing work.

Ask what correction route applies to this particular work

Describe the condition to the permitting authority before choosing a repair strategy. A garage used as a separate dwelling raises different questions from a relocated sink. In the City of San Diego, City of San Diego: Legalizing Unpermitted ADUs (IB 242) describes a specific process for qualifying unpermitted ADUs. It is not blanket permission to keep every unpermitted addition or conversion.

Ask which drawings, evidence of construction date, inspections and corrections are needed. If there is an enforcement notice, give the project team the complete notice and case number. Do not treat a new application as a reason to disregard its deadlines or directions.

Leave room in the scope for investigation

An estimate based only on visible finishes cannot confirm hidden foundations, wiring or drainage. Identify what the designer or inspector needs to see and how openings will be made and repaired. Separate the investigation from the final corrective work so the first authorization does not pretend to settle unknown costs.

For example, a planned cabinet installation may depend on keeping a wall that was moved years ago. Confirming that wall’s role and approval history before fabricating cabinets is more useful than adding a vague “unforeseen conditions” line to the proposal.

Compare correction with redesign

Once requirements are known, compare retaining and correcting the space with revising the remodel to avoid relying on it. Include drawings, engineering, access, restoration and the effect on the rest of the house. There is no reliable universal fee or percentage that makes all old work legal.

ADELIE can discuss construction scope and sequencing. Questions about a purchase disclosure, insurance coverage or a dispute with a seller belong with the appropriate real-estate, insurance or legal adviser.

Keep the final record, not just the application

At completion, retain the approved plans, corrections and final inspection status for the authorized work. A receipt for an application documents submission; it does not establish that construction was accepted. Keep a clear list of anything outside the corrected scope so a future project does not repeat the same uncertainty.

Questions homeowners ask

Can I just apply for the permit after the remodel?

That can leave you needing to expose or change finished work. Establish the required process before continuing the affected construction. The authority determines what can be approved and how it must be verified.

Does every unpermitted room have to be demolished?

There is no single answer. The correction options depend on the work, location and applicable requirements. Do not accept a promise that every condition can be legalized, or assume removal is necessary before the property has been evaluated.

Sources and local requirements
